1 · The need

A morning in a government OPD

The registration counter opens and the corridor is already full. Most of the problems below are familiar to anyone who has stood in that corridor.

What patients face

  • Language. A Marathi-speaking grandmother, a Hindi-speaking labourer and an English-speaking student meet the same doctor. Describing symptoms well in a second language is hard, and details get lost.
  • Literacy. Many patients cannot fill a form. Paper slips and long questionnaires exclude them.
  • Uncertainty. Nobody knows their turn or how long the wait is, so everyone crowds the doctor's door and no one can step away.
  • Fairness. "Why was that person called before me?" — without an answer, trust falls and arguments rise.

What clinicians face

  • Very little time per patient. Much of it goes on basic questions — since when, how bad, which medicines, any allergies — before real examination starts.
  • Unstructured history. The story arrives in pieces, in different languages, with nothing written down.
  • Hidden urgency. A patient with a warning sign can sit quietly in the line behind twenty routine visits.
  • No picture of the day. Administrators cannot see waiting times, department load or language mix to plan staff.

The problem we set out to solve

Patient history-taking for crowded OPDs: collect a structured patient history before the consultation, in the patient's language, and help staff manage the OPD queue — without diagnosing or recommending treatment.

What a good answer has to do

  1. Take history in the patient's language, in a way that works for people who cannot read.
  2. Catch danger signs immediately, not at the end.
  3. Hand the doctor a short, structured English summary — with the patient's own words a click away.
  4. Make the queue live, fair and explainable, and tell patients where they stand.
  5. Be safe, private and affordable for public hospitals: no diagnosis, encrypted data, works on ordinary hardware and without reliable internet.
The problem is not a lack of doctors' skill — it is the minutes lost to collecting history, the language gap, and a queue nobody can see. OPD Saathi gives those minutes back.

4 · End-to-end workflow

Sunita's visit — 9:12 to 9:16 at the kiosk

Sunita, 34, has had fever and cough for three days. She speaks Marathi and reads little English. Follow her through the system — and see what happens behind each screen.

Behind the scenes The kiosk is a browser that was set up once with a one-time activation link and keeps itself signed in with its own device key. Pressing Start asks the FastAPI server to open a visit; the server checks the kiosk is still in service, creates the visit in PostgreSQL and a short-lived session in Redis. The consent text is versioned and hashed, so the exact words she agreed to are recorded. The screen is read aloud by Sarvam Bulbul (natural Indian voices), or by the computer's own voice when there is no internet.
Behind the scenes Had she tapped "Chest discomfort", the server would raise an urgent red flag, write it to the database and publish it instantly to every nurse's screen over a WebSocket — before a single history question is asked. Names and phone numbers are encrypted with AES-256-GCM before they are stored.
4 · End-to-end workflow (continued)

Questions that adapt, and a history she confirms

Behind the scenes The interview engine builds 8–15 questions from 13 topic modules (fever, breathing, stomach, pain, urine, skin, injury, sleep, past illness, medicines, allergies, family, other) and skips what does not apply — no pregnancy questions for men or children. When she speaks, her browser streams 16 kHz audio over a WebSocket to the server, which relays it to Sarvam Saaras (real-time Indian-language speech recognition); her words appear on screen for her to check. With a key, Google Gemini picks the most useful next question and may add one short follow-up — always validated against the question bank. 15 safety rules scan every answer in every language: "blood in phlegm" would raise a flag immediately.
Behind the scenes Confirming runs four things at once: the routing engine picks the department from her complaint (keyword rules in all three languages, with paediatric routing under 14); the triage scorer computes her 0–100 score; a case sheet is assembled in English with her original Marathi kept beside each field; and the visit is placed in the department's queue — a Redis sorted set updated by an atomic Lua script — with the next daily token number (MED-016).
Sunita answered in Marathi, mostly by tapping, and heard her own history read back before confirming it. Four minutes later the doctor has it in English.
4 · End-to-end workflow (continued)

9:16 — Her phone, and the nurse's screen

Behind the scenes The phone page holds an open Server-Sent Events stream, so it updates by itself the moment the queue moves — no refresh, no app, no login, and no personal or health details on the page. The nurse's screen uses a WebSocket: when any visit changes, the server writes the change to an outbox table in the same database transaction, the ARQ worker relays it through Redis pub/sub within about two seconds, and every open staff screen updates. If Redis were ever lost, the whole queue is rebuilt from PostgreSQL. The estimated wait is a five-minute range (for example 15–20 min), calculated from the last 20 consultation times and the number of doctors working.

What the nurse can do

  • Call the next patient, start and complete consultations, mark no-shows.
  • Acknowledge alerts: red flags, "Patient asked for help", "AI suggests this patient may be more urgent — please confirm".
  • Change priority — only with a written reason, kept in the audit log.
  • Confirm the department when the routing was unsure; register a patient who needs help.

Built for a busy desk

  • Four counters (Waiting, Called, In consultation, Priority/urgent) double as filters.
  • Search by token or complaint; optional alert sound; works on phones too.
  • A 5,000-patient list applies an urgent change in about 22 ms (virtualised list).
  • Unfinished visits from earlier days close automatically overnight.
4 · End-to-end workflow (continued)

9:41 — The doctor's case sheet

Behind the scenes The case sheet is stored as validated JSON with a version number. Every doctor edit creates a new version marked Staff-verified; if two people edit at once, the second is told instead of silently overwriting (optimistic concurrency with ETags). The PDF is rendered on the server by WeasyPrint with Indian-language fonts. The AI summary is produced by Gemini only from her answers, passed through an output guard that rejects any diagnosis, treatment advice, dose or link — and it stays labelled unverified until a person checks it.
The doctor opens one page: urgent warnings first, an English summary, and Sunita's own Marathi words one click away. Every correction is versioned and audited.
4 · End-to-end workflow (continued)

End of day — reports and administration

Behind the scenes Reports read from a pre-computed PostgreSQL materialized view that the worker refreshes every five minutes, so even 100,000 visits summarise in about 2 ms. Filters for period and department drive every card, the chart and the CSV export together. "Estimated time saved" is clearly labelled as an estimate (visits × the minutes the hospital assumes each kiosk interview saves).

The administrator adds doctors, nurses and departments, registers each kiosk and sets it up by opening one link on the device. Kiosks report in every minute; a replaced device gets a new code for the same kiosk, keeping its history. Staff, kiosks and departments can be turned off or deleted — deleting never erases medical history.

6 · The queuing algorithm

Fair, explainable, and impossible to game

A simple "first come, first served" line ignores the sick. A pure "sickest first" line can make a routine patient wait all day. OPD Saathi uses a virtual arrival time: each patient is placed as if they had arrived a little earlier — by an amount their triage score earns, with a hard limit.

Queue position = arrival time − head start  ·  head start = 0.6 minutes × score, never more than 45 minutes  ·  urgent patients go to the front.

Step 1 — the triage score (0–100, staff only)

  • Red flags: urgent flag or danger tile 70; priority flag 30; +5 for each further flag (max +15).
  • Warning answers tapped by the patient: blood in phlegm +25, blood in urine +12, breathless +10, very little urine +10… all together at most 25.
  • Vulnerability: infant under 1 +20, child 1–4 +10, aged 75+ +12, 65–74 +8, pregnant +12.
  • Onset: started today +6, 1–3 days +3.
  • Self-rating 7–10/10: only +1 to +4, and nothing for problems lasting months.

Step 2 — level and head start

  • Urgent any urgent red flag or score ≥ 70 → front of the queue, in arrival order.
  • Priority score 30–69 → head start 18–41 minutes.
  • Routine score below 30 → head start 0–17 minutes.
  • The level bands guarantee a nurse's override always wins over the points.
  • AI is a checker: an AI flag counts as priority at most, can only raise urgency, and must quote the patient.

6 · The queuing algorithm (continued)

Explainable on screen, fast in memory

Estimated wait, honestly

  • Average consultation = mean of the last 20 consultations in that department (default 4 minutes until five exist), bounded to 1–15 minutes.
  • Baseline = (patients ahead) × average ÷ doctors working.
  • Shown as a range — −25 % to +25 %, rounded to 5 minutes — so we never promise false precision. Example: 4 ahead, 2 doctors, 8-minute consultations → "15–20 min".

How it runs

  • Each department's queue is a Redis sorted set ordered by virtual time — insert and reorder in O(log n).
  • Changes are applied by atomic Lua scripts, so two nurses clicking at once can never corrupt the order.
  • PostgreSQL stays the source of truth; an outbox guarantees every change reaches Redis and the screens, and the queue can be rebuilt from the database at any time.
  • Urgent changes reach staff screens within about two seconds; a 5,000-row list re-renders in ~22 ms.
Our queue is fair and explainable: urgent first, a bounded head start for the vulnerable and the unwell, a hard 45-minute cap so no one waits forever, a self-rating that can't cheat — and a "Why?" for every position.
7 · AI and voice

AI that assists, never decides

AI makes OPD Saathi smarter in the patient's language — but every core feature works without it, and it is never allowed to diagnose, advise, or lower urgency.

ServiceWhat it does in OPD SaathiWithout it

Google Gemini
Flash-Lite models, structured JSON output		Translates free-text answers to English; writes the doctor's 2–3 line brief; chooses the most useful next question and short follow-ups; reads old prescription photos; gives a second opinion on warning signs; reviews the triage after check-in.Question bank order, bilingual answer bank for translation, safety rules only.

Guard-rails on every AI call

  • Names and identifiers are removed before anything is sent; prompts and replies are not logged.
  • Answers must match a strict JSON schema and are validated again on our side.
  • An output guard rejects any text that diagnoses, advises treatment, gives a dose or contains a link.
  • AI red flags must quote the patient's actual words, count as priority at most, and only ever raise urgency.
  • 2.5-second deadline, one retry, a circuit breaker, and at most 30 calls per check-in — if AI is slow or down, the kiosk quietly continues with the question bank.

Why these providers

  • Sarvam is an Indian AI company whose speech models are trained for Indian languages, accents and code-mixed speech — exactly our patients. Its real-time stream shows words as they are spoken.
  • Gemini Flash-Lite is fast and low-cost, supports guaranteed JSON output, and handles Hindi and Marathi well — suited to many short tasks per patient.
  • Every provider sits behind a small adapter, so a hospital can switch providers — or run with none — without touching the rest of the system.
  • AI is switched on per installation with keys, and each hospital can opt out.
AI translates, summarises and double-checks — but rules decide, AI can only add caution, staff confirm, and the hospital can run the whole system with AI switched off.
8 · Security and privacy

Built for health data, layer by layer

LayerHow OPD Saathi protects it
Data at restNames, phone numbers, answers and case sheets are encrypted field by field with AES-256-GCM, each value bound to its own row and column so it cannot be copied elsewhere. Phone lookups use a keyed HMAC-SHA256 digest, never the number itself.
Hospital isolationPostgreSQL row-level security is forced on all 17 tables: the database itself refuses to return another hospital's rows, even if the application had a bug. Verified live: a second hospital could not read, call, change or delete anything of the first.
Staff sign-inPasswords hashed with Argon2id. Signed 10-minute access tokens (JWT) kept only in memory; a rotating refresh cookie that is httpOnly and SameSite=Strict, with reuse detection that revokes the whole session. Five wrong passwords lock the account for 15 minutes.
KiosksOne-time activation codes that expire after 24 hours and are stored only as hashes; each kiosk then holds its own device key. Turning a kiosk off, deleting it or re-pairing it cuts the old device off immediately.
Access controlFive roles (admin, doctor, nurse, kiosk, patient); every one of the 55 private API endpoints checks the role; a patient token sees only its own visit; nobody can lock themselves out.
Abuse and mistakesRate limits on sign-in and kiosk activation (Redis sliding window); every change carries an idempotency key, so a double tap or retried request never creates two visits; strict input validation and request-size limits; CSV exports protected from spreadsheet formula tricks.
BrowserA strict Content-Security-Policy, no framing, no-sniff and no-referrer headers; staff tokens never stored in the browser; the offline form is encrypted on the device with a key that cannot be exported.
AccountabilityAn append-only audit log records sign-ins, edits, overrides (with reasons), kiosk activations and admin changes. Deleting a record archives it, so medical history is never lost.
SecretsEach installation generates its own passwords, encryption and signing keys; production start-up refuses weak or default keys.
Every change, checkedGitHub Actions run bandit and semgrep code scanning, pip-audit and npm audit dependency checks and gitleaks secret scanning; code cannot reach the main branch unless all pass.

Privacy by design

Only age and sex are required. The phone status page shows no name or health details. Patient data stays on the hospital's own server. An optional retention tool removes old transcripts and pseudonymises patients.

Clinical safety by design

No diagnosis, disease names, medicines or advice anywhere. Danger signs first. Uncertainty always escalates. The patient confirms the read-back; doctors can correct anything.

9 · Scalability and reliability

From one kiosk to many hospitals

Many kiosks, one queue

Any number of kiosks per hospital feed the same department queues. Tokens are numbered per department per day, whichever kiosk was used. Each kiosk shows its hospital's name and reports online/offline every minute.

Many hospitals, fully separate

Each hospital gets its own installation and database — set up with one command. Several hospitals can also share one server (for example a district hosting its PHCs), separated by the database's row-level security.

Built to scale out

  • The API is stateless: run more copies behind a load balancer.
  • Queues in Redis sorted sets (O(log n)); reports from a pre-computed view.
  • Virtualised lists keep staff screens fast with thousands of rows.
  • Two Redis instances: durable state (append-only file) and a separate, size-bounded cache.

Built not to break

  • Outbox pattern: queue changes are written in the same transaction as the visit, then delivered — nothing is lost if a service restarts.
  • The live queue can be rebuilt from PostgreSQL at any time.
  • Idempotency keys stop duplicate visits from retries.
  • 5-second database statement timeout; services restart automatically.
  • Offline kiosk form when the network fails.

Works where the internet does not

SituationWhat happens
Internet down, hospital network upEverything keeps working — check-in, queue, case sheets, reports — because kiosks talk to the hospital's own server. Only AI, natural voices and voice typing pause; patients tap or type.
Kiosk cannot reach the serverThe app is cached on the kiosk (PWA service worker), so it still opens. Within about 10 seconds it offers a short form, encrypted on the device (AES-256-GCM, IndexedDB), with a provisional token. It syncs automatically when the link returns — never creating a duplicate.
Slow networksThe app is downloaded once and cached; afterwards only small JSON messages travel. Kiosks stay signed in through outages.

Measured

  • Reports summary over 100,000 visits: 1.88 ms (95th percentile) on a laptop.
  • 5,000-patient queue: urgent update applied in about 22 ms.
  • Lighthouse accessibility 100 on kiosk and status pages.
Honest note: these are laptop measurements and a small load smoke test, not a certified capacity figure. The path to larger scale (connection pooling, read replicas, managed database) is documented.
10 · Design

Designed for people under pressure

For patients

  • One question per screen, big tappable answers (minimum 64 × 64 px).
  • A six-step progress bar on every screen: Agree → Details → Safety check → Questions → Review → Token.
  • Everything read aloud; high-contrast mode; plain, neutral wording.
  • Mistakes are easy to undo: Back, Change, Stop and exit.

For staff

  • What matters first: red flags at the top, one main action per patient.
  • Same layout on laptop, tablet and phone; never scrolls sideways (checked at 7 widths from 320 to 1600 px).
  • Clear status colours, counters that filter, and confirmation before anything risky.

Accessible and inclusive

  • Zero serious accessibility issues in automated (axe) scans.
  • All colour pairs pass WCAG AA contrast — checked in CI.
  • About 830 translated phrases per language, kept in sync by a CI check.

Clean engineering

  • Clear modules: interview engine, safety rules, routing, triage, queue, case sheets — each testable on its own.
  • Every AI and voice provider behind a small adapter.
  • Typed end to end: the API's schema generates the web app's TypeScript types.
  • Rules and weights in readable files for clinicians to review.

12 · Deployment

How a hospital gets OPD Saathi

Each hospital gets its own installation on a PC or server inside the hospital. Patient data never leaves the building, and the OPD keeps running when the internet does not.

  1. Prepare a server on the hospital network with Docker; give it a fixed local address.
  2. Run one command — .\setup-hospital.ps1. It creates fresh secrets and encryption keys, builds and starts the database, Redis, API, worker and web app, and asks for the hospital's name and first administrator. It also creates the 8 standard departments.
  3. The administrator signs in and adds doctors, nurses and departments in Settings.
  4. Each kiosk is set up by registering it and opening its setup link once on the device; it then stays signed in and appears as Online.
  5. Optional: add Gemini and Sarvam keys for AI and natural voices; set up HTTPS for voice on every kiosk; schedule backups.

One installation per hospital (recommended)

Own server, own database, own keys. Best for privacy and for hospitals with unreliable internet.

Several hospitals on one server

For example a district hosting its PHCs. Each hospital is added with one command and separated by row-level security.

16 · For the presentation (continued)

Questions the judges may ask

QuestionAnswer
Does it diagnose?No — by design. It collects history and flags warning signs for staff. An output guard blocks any AI text that diagnoses or advises treatment.
What if the AI is wrong?AI can only add caution: its flags count as priority at most, must quote the patient, and staff confirm. Safety rules decide. The AI summary is marked unverified until a person checks it.
What if there is no internet?Kiosks talk to the hospital's own server, so check-in and the queue keep working. If even the local network fails, the kiosk switches to an encrypted offline form that syncs later.
Can someone jump the queue?No. The self-rating is worth at most 4 of 100 points; priority comes from real signs, age and pregnancy; the head start is capped at 45 minutes; overrides need a written reason and are audited.
How is patient data protected?Field-level AES-256-GCM encryption, per-hospital row-level security in the database, Argon2id passwords, short-lived tokens, an audit log, and data that stays on the hospital's own server.
How does it scale?Any number of kiosks feed one queue per department; queues live in Redis sorted sets; the API is stateless and can be replicated; each hospital is a separate installation set up with one command.
What does it cost to run?The stack is open-source and runs on an ordinary PC. Without AI there is no per-patient cost; AI adds small per-call costs on fast, low-cost models, and each hospital can switch it off.
What about people who cannot use a kiosk?Voice, big buttons and Call staff help most; anyone else is registered by a nurse with the same consent and safety steps.
Why Sarvam and Gemini?Sarvam's speech models are built for Indian languages and accents with real-time streaming; Gemini Flash-Lite is fast, low-cost and returns strict JSON. Both sit behind adapters and can be replaced.
Is it deployed?It is designed to be installed per hospital on its own server — one command (setup-hospital.ps1). Today we show it on a laptop, exactly as it would run on a hospital PC.
What's next?Clinician review, a one-department pilot, doctor's notes, ABHA/ABDM integration and more languages.

OPD Saathi collects history for clinical staff. It does not diagnose or recommend treatment. All patients, staff and hospitals shown in this document are fictional.
